01 · Executive summary

The verticals are already a filter. The product just doesn’t know it yet.

Calendars has one dataset, two presentations, four configuration domains and two different app shells. The fastest route to a coherent architecture is not to add structure — it is to stop modelling the same idea in three incompatible ways.

The decisive evidence is in your own screenshots. Both Calendar view and Appointment list view carry a “Services” dropdown in the toolbar. That control is a vertical selector. Meanwhile, in Calendar settings, those same verticals appear as top-level tabs (Meetings, Services, Rentals, Connections).

So the product already treats a vertical as a filter in the operational plane and a destination in the configuration plane. The unified-calendar mechanism you are proposing to build largely exists — it is just implemented as a single-select scope switch instead of a multi-select facet. That reframes the work substantially: this is less a new feature than the correction of a modelling error, which materially lowers the risk of shipping it to a surface with 1.5m monthly actives.

What I agree with

  • Appointments as the primary workspace, with Calendar and List as view types of one dataset. Correct, and supported by near-universal industry convergence.
  • Saved Views should belong to the dataset, not the table. Correct diagnosis. Your proposed model needs one structural fix (below).
  • A unified calendar is right, and “All” should be the default.

Where I disagree

  • “Meetings” is the wrong name, but renaming the vertical is the wrong fix. The collision is one level down: the word “calendar” currently means four different things. Fix the children, keep the parent.
  • Statuses are not platform-wide. They encode an appointment lifecycle (no-show, showed) that is meaningless for other objects. Keep them in Calendars.
  • Connections are neither purely platform nor purely calendar. They must be split: account identity is platform, sync behaviour is Calendars.
  • Verticals as flat top-level nav items will not scale — not because of nav length, but because each vertical drags a duplicate copy of Staff, Locations and Resources behind it. This is the most expensive latent problem in your proposed tree.
  • The vertical should not be the headline filter in the unified calendar. Operators think staff-first and day-first. Type is one facet among several, not the marquee control.

The one structural change I would insist on

Extract shared resources before adding verticals

Staff, Locations and Resources are shared objects that every vertical references. Today, Services owns its own Staff / Resources / Locations tabs. If Rentals and Home Services each grow their own, Jane Doe gets configured four times, availability conflicts become unresolvable, and the unified calendar cannot compute a trustworthy busy state. Extract them once, reference them everywhere.

FactWe have Pendo instrumentation showing scale (251k accounts, 1.5m MAU), mid-range NPS (~47) with a recent detractor spike, 10+ distinct booking entry points, active mobile usage on iOS and Android, and a live A/B test on feature discoverability.

AssumptionWe have no user interviews and no usability testing for this problem space. Every mental-model claim in this document is reasoned from role incentives, the product’s own telemetry and comparable-product evidence — not from research with users. Section 22 proposes how to close that gap before the expensive phases ship.

02 · Current-state analysis

What the screenshots actually reveal

Reading the six screens as an architecture rather than as a set of layouts.

The three-tab header is not a hierarchy

The Calendars header presents three peers: Calendar view, Appointment list view, Calendar settings. The first two are presentations of the same records. The third is a portal into a completely different app shell — a Settings left nav with a “← Go Back” escape hatch. One tab bar is doing two incompatible jobs: switching a view, and teleporting between shells. Users cannot form a stable model of “where am I” because the answer changes category depending on which tab they press.

Four referents, one word, all reachable within three clicks of each other. This is the single largest terminology defect and it compounds every other navigation problem.

The scope selector is hidden in plain sight

In Calendar view the “Services” dropdown sits between Week view and the display-options button — visually grouped with view controls. In List view the same dropdown sits immediately beside the Appointments heading — visually grouped with the page title. Identical function, two different semantic placements, so users learn it twice. And because it is single-select, the product structurally cannot answer “what is happening today across my whole business?”

Configuration outweighs operations 4:2

The product gives four top-level tabs to configuration domains (Meetings, Services, Rentals, Connections) and two to daily work. HypothesisTask frequency is inverted relative to this allocation: seeing today’s schedule and finding, creating or editing an appointment happen many times per day, while creating a service or connecting a calendar happens at setup and occasionally after. Every comparable product we examined (Square, GoDaddy, Acuity, Wix, Time2book) places the operational calendar first and pushes catalogue configuration below it.

Notable details worth preserving

  • Buffer-time visibility as a display toggle is a genuinely domain-specific control with no analogue in generic SaaS. Keep it.
  • “New (action required)” rendered as a red-outlined editable dropdown in the list is excellent: status is both the alarm and the control. This should become the model for status everywhere.
  • Groups on the calendars list (105 ungrouped of 122) shows real-world scale and a grouping mechanism users are already under-using — a discoverability signal consistent with the live Pendo experiment.
03 · Core UX problems

Ranked by severity

P0 = fundamental, blocks a coherent model. P1 = major, causes daily friction or blocks scale. P2 = moderate. P3 = minor.

P0

One dataset is modelled as two destinations

Calendar view and Appointment list view are presentations, not places. Making them navigation peers forces every dataset-level concept — filters, saved configurations, search, bulk actions, status — to be built, learned and maintained twice, and guarantees they drift apart. They already have.

P0

“Calendar” has four distinct referents

Product, view, booking configuration, and external synced account all share one word within three clicks of each other. No amount of layout improvement compensates for a collapsed vocabulary; users cannot describe to each other where they are.

P0

A tab bar that switches app shells

“Calendar settings” presents as a peer of two view tabs but transports the user into a different navigation frame requiring “Go Back” to escape. Navigation must answer “where am I” consistently; here the same control class produces two categories of outcome.

P1

Verticals are simultaneously a filter and a destination

Single-select scope dropdown in the operational plane; top-level tabs in the configuration plane. Users must hold two contradictory models of what a vertical is, and the business question “what is happening across everything today?” is structurally unanswerable.

P1

Shared objects are owned by verticals

Staff, Locations and Resources live inside Services. Adding Rentals and Home Services on the same pattern produces N duplicate copies of the same real-world entities, breaks cross-vertical conflict detection, and makes a trustworthy unified busy-state impossible to compute.

P1

Saved configurations exist on only one side

Smart Lists are bound to the table representation. The most valuable personalisation in the product is unavailable in the view most people open first, and cannot be shared, defaulted or governed consistently.

P1

Configuration outranks operations in the IA

Four configuration tabs, two operational ones. Frequent tasks pay a navigation cost to subsidise infrequent ones — the opposite of what task-frequency analysis and every comparable product suggests.

P2

Two filter vocabularies and two entry points

“Manage view” (right panel, mixes filters with display options and type scope) versus “Advanced filters” (chip, count badge). The Calendar panel also conflates three distinct concepts — view-by-type, display toggles, and entity filters — in one undifferentiated stack.

P2

“Meetings” under-describes its contents

The vertical contains Event, Personal, Round robin and Class booking types. A 7am yoga class is not a meeting. The label narrows a general booking-calendar capability and will keep drifting as types are added.

P2

“Service calendars (v1)” leaks versioning into the UI

Migration state is exposed as a permanent navigation label. Users must understand the product’s release history to choose a destination.

P3

Undifferentiated “Busy” blocks

The grid is dominated by identical grey “Busy” blocks carrying only a time range. High ink, low information — and it pre-empts the visual budget the unified calendar will need for type and status encoding.

P3

Grouping is under-adopted

105 of 122 calendars are ungrouped. The mechanism exists but is not discovered or not worth the effort — consistent with the discoverability problem the live Pendo experiment is measuring.

The load-bearing conclusion

Four of five roles do not think in verticals at all. Only the agency admin does — and that role spends its time in configuration, not the calendar. This is the strongest argument for the unified calendar, and simultaneously the strongest argument against making vertical the headline filter control. The vertical is an artefact of how the business was configured, not how the day is experienced.

How each role reads the current vocabulary

  • “Appointments” — understood consistently by everyone. The safest anchor word in the product.
  • “Calendar” — read as the grid by operators, as a bookable configuration by admins. This split is the terminology defect made visible.
  • “Meetings” — read as a scheduled conversation with a person. Operators of class-based businesses will not look here for their yoga schedule.
  • “Services” — strong and unambiguous. Keep.
  • “Rentals” — strong and unambiguous. Keep.
  • “Views” — Assumptionnot a concept most SMB operators hold spontaneously. Slack ships an in-product explainer for exactly this reason; we will need one too.
05 · Entity model

One transaction object, four bookable subtypes

The data model is what makes the unified calendar either coherent or arbitrary. It is coherent.

Appointment — the single operational record; everything users "see" on a calendar │ id, start, end, timezone, status, notes, source, created_by │ recurrence?, buffer_before?, buffer_after? │ ├── bookable → Bookable (polymorphic — this is the keystone) ├── customer → Contact ├── assignees → Staff[] (round-robin, collective, class instructor) ├── location → Location? (fixed, virtual, or customer address) └── resources → Resource[] (room, chair, equipment, unit of stock) Bookable — abstract supertype. Four concrete subtypes today: ├── BookingCalendar 1:1 · round robin · collective · class · event ├── Service duration, price band, category, add-ons, staff eligibility ├── RentalListing rate unit (hour/day/week/month), stock count, category └── HomeService service area, travel time, on-site duration Shared objects — referenced by every Bookable subtype ├── Staff availability, eligibility, per-staff calendar connection ├── Location physical / virtual / customer-site ├── Resource finite capacity, bookable in parallel with staff ├── Category merchandising + filter grouping, per Bookable type └── Status appointment lifecycle state machine Platform objects — owned outside Calendars ├── Contact the customer record ├── User/Role permissions └── Connection OAuth identity (see §16 — this one splits)

Grey text is annotation, not structure.

Why this validates the unified calendar

Every vertical produces the same object: an Appointment with a bookable reference and a bookable.type. There is no schema difference between a haircut, a discovery call and a villa booking at the operational layer — only a difference in which subtype the bookable points at. A unified calendar is therefore not a merge of dissimilar things; it is a query over one table with the type constraint removed. That is a strong architectural signal that the unified view is the natural state and the current per-vertical scoping is the imposed one.

What the tree tells us

  • Depth to a daily task is 1–2. Good, and worth protecting.
  • Depth to a configuration task is 4–5, across a shell boundary: Calendars → Calendar settings → Services → Staff. Each hop changes the navigation frame’s meaning.
  • Four concepts are interleaved in one panel. Manage view mixes filters, display options and type scope with no visual separation — the clearest single instance of the concept-mixing this project set out to fix.
  • The Settings shell hosts a second, contradictory model of verticals. Same nouns, different grammar.

I · Multi-type visual encoding

Only two references in this board differentiate entity types without relying on hue, and both matter disproportionately: HoneyBook’s hatched-vs-solid fill for tentative-vs-confirmed, and Confluence’s per-type icons. Everything else in the board — Amie, Front, Motion, Rise, HoneyBook’s own type dots — encodes with colour alone. HypothesisThis is a genuine gap in the market rather than a solved problem, which means we should not expect to find a pattern to copy and should design the encoding system deliberately (§12).

J · What the board does not support

  • No product in this board exposes business verticals as top-level navigation. Square, GoDaddy, Acuity, Wix and Time2book all organise by object class or audience. That is a meaningful absence given how many multi-offering scheduling products were sampled.
  • No product ships more than three primary view types over a calendar dataset. ClickUp’s eleven tabs are the visible failure mode.
  • Only one product (Wix) splits Calendar and List into nav siblings — and it duplicates its filter panel as a result.
08 · Pattern synthesis

What the evidence converges on

Six conclusions

  1. The four-part header grammar is settled. Dataset title → view type → named saved view → filter chips, each with a distinct control shape. Motion, Height, GitHub, Airtable and Todoist all land within a small variation of this. We should adopt it rather than invent.
  2. Dirty state is the mechanism that makes saved views usable. Without it, users either fear touching a saved view or accidentally overwrite a shared one. Height’s three-exit contract (Revert / Save as / Save) plus Zapier’s contextual placement is the complete solution.
  3. Filter legibility beats filter power. Airtable naming the active filter in the chip is worth more than condition groups. For a system of record, a silently-filtered calendar is a trust failure — the user concludes appointments have vanished.
  4. Type belongs in a toolbar pill, not a sidebar. Amie’s count-bearing pill preserves grid width, scales via search, and states its own state. Our existing right-hand Manage view panel should keep the long-tail dimensions and hand the type facet to the toolbar.
  5. Two view types, hard cap. Calendar and List. ClickUp demonstrates the failure of unbounded types; nothing in the board suggests a third earns its place for scheduling.
  6. Creation resolves type through the bookable, not through a mode switch. Time2book’s grouped picker is better than Square’s explicit type field because it removes a decision while still teaching the taxonomy.

Design principles

Eight principles to govern the redesign. Where they refine the brief’s starting hypothesis, I say so.

Principle 01

Navigation names places; everything else names lenses

A destination is somewhere you can bookmark and describe to a colleague. A view type, a filter, a date range and a saved view are all lenses onto a place. The brief’s starting hypothesis holds — with one refinement: display options are a fifth category, and they belong with the view type, not with filters.

Principle 02

One dataset, one workspace

Any concept that applies to the appointment dataset — search, filters, saved views, status, bulk actions, permissions — is built once and works in every presentation. If a capability exists in only one view, that is a bug, not a roadmap item.

Principle 03

Frequency earns proximity

Daily tasks get zero-click or one-click access; monthly tasks can afford two; setup tasks can afford three. The current 4:2 split favouring configuration inverts this and is the reason configuration feels like the product’s centre of gravity.

Principle 04

Shared objects are configured once

Staff, Locations and Resources are real-world entities, not per-vertical settings. Every vertical references them. This is the principle that most constrains the IA and the one most likely to be violated under delivery pressure.

Principle 05

Never encode meaning in colour alone

Every distinction an operator must make under time pressure — type, status, certainty — must survive greyscale, colour-blindness and a 40%-brightness laptop at a reception desk. Glyph, border style and fill pattern carry the load; colour amplifies.

Principle 06

State is always legible without opening a panel

If a view is filtered, the toolbar says what by. If a saved view has drifted, the toolbar says so. If a range is empty because of a filter rather than because nothing is booked, the empty state says which. Hidden state destroys trust in a system of record.

Principle 07

The default must be correct for the single-vertical account

AssumptionMost accounts run one vertical. The unified calendar must therefore cost them nothing — no extra control to dismiss, no type column consuming space, no empty legend. Multi-vertical capability should be invisible until a second vertical exists.

Principle 08

Preserve domain-specific controls that generic SaaS lacks

Buffer-time visibility, blocked slots, unified service view, capacity and look-busy have no analogue in project-management tools. Their absence from the Mobbin board is not evidence they are wrong — it is evidence our domain is more specific. Do not simplify them away in pursuit of a familiar pattern.

09 · IA alternatives

Four models, evaluated

Option D emerged from the entity model and is not in the brief. It is the one I recommend.

Option A — Appointments-first, verticals as siblings (the brief’s hypothesis)

Calendars ├── Appointments → Calendar | List ├── Meetings ├── Services ├── Home Services ├── Rentals └── Settings (Connections, Statuses)

Mental model: “My schedule, then the things I offer, then how it all works.”

  • Strengths: Correctly separates operations from configuration. Direct access to each vertical. Minimal conceptual leap from today’s Settings tabs, so migration reads as a promotion rather than a restructure.
  • Weaknesses: Nav grows linearly with verticals. Critically, it provides no home for shared objects — Staff, Locations and Resources must either live inside each vertical (duplication) or inside Settings (wrong: they are operational catalogue data, edited weekly, not system configuration). This is the flaw that disqualifies it as-is.
  • Scalability: Poor past ~5 verticals. Discoverability: Good. Ops efficiency: Good. Config efficiency: Poor — configuring Jane Doe means visiting N verticals. Migration: Low. Verdict: Right instinct, incomplete object model.

Option B — Vertical-first

Calendars ├── Meetings → Calendar | List | Setup ├── Services → Calendar | List | Setup ├── Rentals → Calendar | List | Setup └── Settings

Mental model: “Pick the part of my business, then work inside it.”

  • Strengths: Each vertical is self-contained and independently shippable — genuinely attractive from a delivery and team-ownership perspective. Matches the agency admin’s mental model. Permissions map cleanly onto verticals.
  • Weaknesses: Structurally forecloses the unified calendar — the primary business question becomes unanswerable by design. Duplicates Calendar, List, filters and saved views N times. Four of five roles do not think in verticals. Staff duplication is not merely likely but mandatory.
  • Scalability: Poor. Discoverability: Poor — “where do I see my day?” has N answers. Ops efficiency: Poor for multi-vertical, fine for single. Config efficiency: Good within a vertical, bad across. Migration: Lowest — it is roughly today’s model. Verdict: Reject. Optimises for the org chart, not the user.

Option C — Hybrid: Appointments + a single catalogue node

Calendars ├── Appointments → Calendar | List ├── Catalogue → Booking calendars | Services | Rentals | Home services ├── Staff & resources └── Settings

Mental model: “My schedule, my offerings, my people, my rules.”

  • Strengths: Nav stays at four items regardless of vertical count — handles the 10-vertical case cleanly. Shared objects get a proper home. Conceptually the tidiest tree.
  • Weaknesses: Adds a click to every configuration task and introduces an abstraction (“Catalogue”) that no user asked for. Hypothesis“Catalogue” is retail vocabulary that a plumber or a law firm will not adopt; the Mobbin board offers no support for it in a scheduling context (Square’s comparable “Library” is equally opaque). A single-vertical salon now navigates Catalogue → Services instead of Services.
  • Scalability: Excellent. Discoverability: Moderate — depends entirely on the parent label landing. Ops efficiency: Good. Config efficiency: Good. Migration: Moderate. Verdict: Structurally strong, linguistically weak.

Option D — Appointments-first with adaptive verticals and extracted shared objects Recommended

Calendars ├── Appointments operational hub — Calendar | List over one dataset │ ├── Booking calendars shown only if the vertical is enabled ├── Services shown only if enabled ├── Rentals shown only if enabled ├── Home services shown only if enabled ├── + Add booking type discovery surface for verticals not yet enabled │ ├── Staff & resources Staff · Locations · Resources — configured ONCE └── Settings Booking pages · Statuses · Calendar sync · Notifications

Mental model: identical to Option A — which is the point. It preserves the brief’s instinct while fixing its two structural gaps.

Two changes do the work:

  1. Verticals render conditionally. A salon sees Appointments / Services / Staff & resources / Settings — four items. An agency sees Appointments / Booking calendars / Staff & resources / Settings. Nobody sees a vertical they do not use. This dissolves Option A’s scalability problem without Option C’s abstraction, because nav length now tracks what the account actually does rather than what the product supports.
  2. Shared objects are extracted into one destination that every vertical references.

The + Add booking type row is a deliberate second job: it converts navigation into the discoverability surface our live Pendo experiment says we need. A salon that has never noticed Rentals exists now encounters it in the one place it is guaranteed to look.

  • Scalability: Excellent — nav length is a function of account configuration, not product surface area. Discoverability: Excellent, and actively improves it. Ops efficiency: Excellent. Config efficiency: Excellent — shared objects configured once. Migration: Moderate — requires the shared-object extraction, which is the real engineering cost. Future verticals: Adding one is a feature flag plus a catalogue screen, with no impact on Appointments.
Why D beats C on the naming question

Option C must find a word that covers booking calendars, services, rentals and home services. Every candidate is either jargon (Catalogue, Library, Offerings) or a category error (Products — these are time, not goods). Option D avoids the problem entirely: it never needs a collective noun, because it never groups them. The cost C was paying to solve scalability, D solves with conditional rendering instead.

12 · Unified calendar

Yes — but type is not the headline

1. Is a unified calendar good UX?

Yes. Three independent lines of evidence agree. The entity model (§5) shows all verticals produce the identical object, so unification is a query, not a merge. The mental models (§4) show four of five roles have no concept of vertical, so per-vertical scoping imposes a boundary users do not hold. And the product itself already ships a single-select vertical scope in both toolbars — the mechanism exists; we are removing a constraint, not adding a feature.

The strongest argument, though, is negative: cross-vertical conflicts are currently invisible. If Jane is booked for a Service at 2pm and a Meeting at 2:15pm, no current screen can show both. That is not a convenience gap, it is a correctness gap in a system of record — and it gets worse with every vertical added.

2–3. Mental model, and when it breaks

HypothesisThe model users hold is “my business’s time” — a single physical reality of who is busy when. The vertical is invisible in that model, which is why unification feels natural rather than clever.

It breaks in three specific situations, each of which needs a designed answer rather than a warning:

  • Different time granularities collide. A 30-minute haircut and a 4-day villa rental cannot share a week grid legibly. Answer: multi-day bookings render in the all-day band at the top, never as tall blocks in the time grid — the same treatment calendars already give all-day events. Rentals therefore read as a horizontal bar spanning days, which is also how the mental model works.
  • Different capacity semantics collide. A class holds 12; a room holds 1. Answer: capacity renders as a fraction on the block (8/12), and the block is only visually “full” when capacity is reached — not when one booking exists.
  • Volume asymmetry. A business with 400 service appointments and 3 rentals per week will find rentals lost in the noise. Answer: this is the case for saved views, not for splitting the calendar — “Rentals only” is one click, and the type chip states that 3 of 403 are visible.

4. Visual differentiation — the encoding system

The Mobbin board (§7I) shows only two products differentiate types without hue. So this must be designed rather than borrowed. Four channels, in priority order:

Thousands of appointments. Density is a rendering problem before it is an IA problem. Mitigations, in order: the range window caps the query (a week is a week regardless of total volume); overlapping blocks collapse to +4 more beyond three per slot; Month granularity shows counts per day rather than blocks; the List view is virtualised and paginated. AssumptionThe genuine risk is not the grid but the query — a cross-vertical union across four tables with staff, location and status predicates needs an index review before “All” ships as default. Permissions. The unified calendar makes a latent problem urgent: it will surface appointments from verticals a user may not be entitled to see. Rules: filter at the query layer, never in the client. A staff member restricted to their own appointments sees only their column, and the type chip shows only types they can access. Where a slot is occupied by an appointment the viewer cannot see, render an opaque busy block with no detail — availability is not confidential, content is. This preserves correct booking behaviour without leaking data, and matches how Google Calendar handles free/busy.

12. Smaller screens

FactPendo shows active iOS and Android usage, so this is not hypothetical. Below 1024px: granularity locks to Day or Schedule (agenda list); staff columns become a horizontally-swipeable set with a sticky staff header; the two toolbar rows collapse into one row plus a single “Filters” sheet containing type, staff and everything else; the saved-view chip stays visible because it is the cheapest way to reach a useful subset on a small screen. Hover-dependent affordances — the dock lift, the edit-nav reveal, tooltips — all need tap equivalents.

13. Creating from the unified calendar

Click-drag a range, or press New appointment. The panel opens with time pre-filled and a single “What are you booking?” picker whose options are grouped by type — Time2book’s pattern, which I prefer to Square’s explicit type field because it removes a decision while still teaching the taxonomy. Choosing a bookable resolves the type, and the form then reveals only that type’s fields: staff and add-ons for a Service, unit and rate period for a Rental, address and travel for a Home service, host and invitees for a Booking calendar. Detail in §17, Flow 7.

13 · Saved Views

Right model, one structural fix

The brief’s instinct — saved configurations belong to the dataset, not to the table — is correct and well supported. But the proposed model, in which a Saved View simply works in both Calendar and List, has a failure mode that will surface within a week of launch.

The failure mode: not every filter set is meaningful in both views

“Today’s appointments” works in both. “Pending appointments” does not. Twelve unconfirmed bookings scattered across three months render as a nearly empty week grid — the user opens their “Pending” view in Calendar, sees nothing, and concludes the view is broken. The mismatch is structural: a status-driven view has no natural date range, while a calendar is defined by one.

Fix: a Saved View stores a preferred view type and each view type’s display config, while the filter set stays shared and canonical. Opening a view lands you in its preferred type; the switcher stays available and remembers your last choice for that view. Airtable establishes exactly this — view type is chosen at creation — and it costs the user nothing.

Disagreement 1 — Statuses are not platform-wide

The brief suspects statuses may belong to the platform. I would argue firmly against it. Appointment statuses encode a lifecycle specific to time-bound bookings: unconfirmed → confirmed → showed / no-show → cancelled. “No-show” is meaningless for an Opportunity, a Contact or an Invoice. Opportunities already have pipeline stages; invoices have payment states. A platform-wide status registry would be an abstraction over three unrelated state machines — it would serve no user and would force every product to describe its states in a vocabulary general enough to be useless.

Evidence: Plane places “Project states” under Workspace › Features — a per-object-type state machine configured at workspace scope but owned by the product that uses it. That is precisely the shape I recommend.

Home: Calendars › Settings › Appointment statuses. Trade-offIf a future requirement genuinely needs cross-object status reporting, the answer is a reporting-layer mapping, not a shared configuration object.

Disagreement 2 — Connections must be split, not relocated

The brief asks whether Connections are platform-wide. Treating them as a single thing is the error — today’s Connections screen bundles two different concerns:

  • Identity: which Google/Microsoft account is authorised. Platform-owned, shared across products, one consent and one revocation path.
  • Behaviour: which of that account’s calendars receives bookings (linked), which are checked for conflicts, and how sync resolves. Purely scheduling, and already staff-scoped in the current UI.

Moving the whole thing to the platform would strand conflict-calendar configuration — an availability-computation input — in a settings area with no scheduling context. Keeping it all in Calendars means users re-authorise Google separately per product.

Resolution: split, and connect the two with Customer.io’s cross-scope banner: on Calendars › Settings › Calendar sync, show “Connected accounts are managed in your account settings”; on the platform integrations page, show “Choose which calendars receive bookings in Calendars › Calendar sync”. Neither surface pretends to own the whole story.

18 · Interaction specifications

The behaviours worth writing down

View switching

  • Preserves filters, range, search, selection, saved view and dirty state. Swaps display config only.
  • Never re-fetches if the underlying query is unchanged — the switch should feel instant, which is also what convinces users it is one dataset.
  • Keyboard: C / L. Range navigation ← / →, T for today, D/W/M for granularity, / for search, N for new.

Filter behaviour

  • Live apply for single-dimension chips (staff, type, status). Batched apply only inside the advanced condition builder, where a partial condition is invalid anyway.
  • Chip labelling: 0 selected → dimension name; 1–2 → the value names; 3+ → “Staff 4”. Naming beats counting whenever it fits, per Airtable.
  • “Clear all” is always present when any filter is active, and does not clear the date range — range is orientation, not a filter.
  • A filter that would produce zero results still applies; the empty state explains which filter is responsible and offers to remove it.

Saved-view dirty state

  • Any change to a stored property marks the view dirty: dot on the chip, tooltip “Modified”.
  • Chip menu when dirty: Revert to saved · Save changes · Save as new view. For shared views, Save is disabled without permission and shows “Ask an admin, or save as a new view” — a dead end with an exit.
  • Navigating away with unsaved changes does not prompt; the dirty state persists in the session and in the URL. Prompting on every navigation in a tool used dozens of times a day would be intolerable.

Calendar direct manipulation

  • Drag to move; edge-drag to resize; both show a live conflict indicator and snap to the slot granularity. Drop outside working hours warns but permits — operators legitimately override.
  • Drag across staff columns reassigns, and checks eligibility: “Sam is not eligible for Gel overlays” with Reassign anyway / Cancel.
  • Every drag is optimistic with a 5-second Undo toast, and reverts visibly on server rejection.
  • Multi-day and all-day items are not vertically resizable — only their day span changes.

Loading, empty and error states

StateTreatment
LoadingGrid chrome and time rules render immediately; blocks arrive as skeletons in place. Never a full-page spinner — the chrome is stable and should not flicker. List uses skeleton rows at the current density.
Empty — nothing booked“No appointments this week.” + New appointment. Quiet.
Empty — because of filters“No appointments match these filters.” Lists the active filters as removable chips, plus “Clear all filters”. This distinction is non-negotiable — conflating the two is how users conclude data is lost.

  • Given the recorded detractor spike, add NPS-verbatim monitoring keyed to scheduling terms for the duration of the rollout, and set a rollback trigger before shipping rather than after.
  • Fact10+ booking entry points are instrumented. Every phase needs regression coverage across all of them — routing, conversation, opportunity, embed, third-party — not just the calendar page. A change that looks safe in the UI can break a booking source that never touches it.

24 · Risks & trade-offs

Where this could go wrong

RiskSeverityMitigation
Unified query performanceHighThe hardest constraint in the proposal. A cross-vertical union with staff, location and status predicates over a large account may not meet interaction latency. Mitigate with an index review before Phase 3, range-bounded queries, per-type parallel fetch with independent failure, and a volume threshold above which All degrades to the most-used types. If this cannot be solved, the unified default must not ship — and that is an acceptable outcome, because Phases 1, 2 and 4 deliver most of the IA value without it.
Calendar block densityHighFour times the information in the same space. Prototype with real high-volume data before committing; keep “Colour by” and a compact/comfortable density toggle as escape valves; be willing to drop capacity fractions from the block to the popover.
Staff deduplication in Phase 5Med-highMerging staff records across verticals can corrupt availability. Admin-reviewed matching, union-not-overwrite for working hours, full rollback per account, and no silent merges.
Saved Views under-adoptedMedium“Views” is not native SMB vocabulary. Three-pronged: seeded views so the feature is populated, contextual save-as-view, in-menu explainer. Measure reuse ratio, not creation count — a feature that is created once and never reopened has failed regardless of adoption numbers.
Two-row header costLow-med~40px of grid height. Collapse row 2 on scroll; validate on a 13-inch laptop with the nav expanded, which is the realistic worst case.
Renames confusing existing usersMedium“Meetings → Booking calendars” and “Smart Lists → Saved Views” both break existing vocabulary and documentation. Ship each with a one-time in-product notice, keep the old term as a search synonym for two releases, and update help content before, not after.
Nav conditional rendering confusing supportLow-medTwo accounts legitimately see different nav, which makes support conversations and documentation harder. Mitigate with a support-tools view of enabled verticals and documentation written around “if you have X enabled”. Scope creep into a view-builder platformLow-medSaved Views invites board views, timelines, dashboards and grouping-by-anything. Hard cap at two view types; any third requires evidence from the reuse-ratio metric, not enthusiasm.

Reading the scores. Option D loses only on migration effort — and it loses there for exactly one reason: the shared-object extraction. That is also the single change that most improves configuration efficiency and future extensibility, so the cost buys the two things the current architecture most lacks. Option C ties D on structure and loses on discoverability and mental-model fit purely on the naming problem (§9). Option A is a genuine improvement over today and is the right thing to ship if the shared-object extraction cannot be funded — note it scores 45, well clear of the current 24, so a partial adoption is still a large win. Option B scores below Option A on almost every user-facing criterion despite being cheapest to build, which is the clearest sign it optimises for delivery convenience.

The recommendation

  1. Adopt Option D. Appointments as the operational hub with Calendar and List as view types; verticals as conditionally-rendered siblings; shared objects extracted; Settings scoped to Calendars with principled cross-links.
  2. Ship the unified calendar with All as default, encoding type by glyph and border, status by colour, with a user-switchable “Colour by”. Gate on the query-performance work.
  3. Build Saved Views as a dataset-level object with a shared filter set, per-view-type display config, and a preferred view type — the one structural correction to the brief’s model.
  4. Rename “Meetings” to “Booking calendars”; keep “Calendars” as the product name. Fix the collision at the child level, where it is cheap.
  5. Keep Statuses in Calendars. Split Connections. Both against the brief’s stated suspicion, for the reasons in §16.
  6. Sequence by blast radius, not coherence (§21). Instrument first; extract shared objects last.
  7. Run the tree test before Phase 1. It is the cheapest study available and it tests the naming decisions this whole tree rests on.
If only one thing ships

Merge Calendar and List into one Appointments workspace with a shared filter model (Phase 1). It fixes two of the three P0 problems, requires no data-model change, makes every subsequent phase cheaper, and is invisible to users who never used more than one view. Everything else in this document is an argument for what to build on top of that foundation.

26 · Open questions

What I could not resolve from the available evidence

  1. How many accounts run more than one vertical? The single most load-bearing unknown in this document. It determines whether the unified calendar is a flagship capability or a niche one, and whether Principle 07’s adaptive defaults are the main experience or an edge case. Answerable from Pendo in Phase 0.
  2. How often is the vertical scope dropdown actually changed? Rarely ⇒ unification is low-risk and mostly invisible. Constantly ⇒ density is the primary design risk and the encoding system needs far more validation.
  3. What is the real Smart List creation and reuse rate? If it is very low today, the Saved Views investment needs its discoverability plan strengthened before Phase 2, not after.
  4. Can the cross-vertical query meet interaction latency on the largest accounts? An engineering question that gates the unified default. It should be answered with a spike, not an estimate.
  5. Is Rentals genuinely time-bookable in the same sense as the others? Multi-day, stock-based, often staff-less. It fits the entity model, but if rental operators do not think of listings as living on a staff-oriented calendar, Rentals may want its own occupancy-grid presentation as a third view type — which would contradict my two-view cap. Worth testing specifically with rental accounts.
  6. Does “Booking calendars” actually test better than “Meetings”? I have argued it from semantics and migration cost, not from evidence. The tree test settles it, and I would change the recommendation if the data disagreed.
  7. Should the platform adopt explicit settings scope tabs? Plane’s pattern would resolve a class of confusion well beyond Calendars, but it is a platform decision outside this project’s remit. Calendars should be designed to work with or without it.
  8. What is causing the recorded NPS detractor spike? Unknown, and it matters for sequencing: if it traces to a recent Calendars change, that regression should be fixed before a restructure lands on top of it.
  9. Where does Waitlist belong? Square treats it as a peer of Calendar. We have no equivalent today. If it arrives, it is a third operational surface — and I would want to know before finalising the Appointments header, since it would compete for row 1.
  10. How do custom objects interact with this? The current left nav shows Pets, Pet Owners and Massage Chairs as top-level items. If custom objects can become bookable, “vertical” stops being a fixed set of four and the adaptive-nav model in Option D becomes essential rather than merely preferable. Worth confirming with the platform team.

Evidence base: six current-product screenshots; the platform left-nav prototype (structure, tokens and motion extracted from its deployed source); Pendo Calendars instrumentation; and 50 screens plus 4 multi-step flows reviewed on Mobbin across 34 products. No user interviews or usability testing were available for this problem space — §22 proposes how to close that gap.
